Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

Complete school + JEE foundation notes for quick learning, revision and problem solving.

1. Introduction

Chemistry is the branch of science that deals with the composition, structure, properties and transformations of matter.

Why is this chapter important?

  • Forms the mathematical foundation of Chemistry.
  • Important for Class XI school examinations.
  • Very important for JEE numerical problems.
  • Introduces the mole concept and stoichiometry.

2. Matter and Its Classification

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

States of Matter

  • Solid: Definite shape and definite volume.
  • Liquid: Definite volume but no definite shape.
  • Gas: Neither definite shape nor definite volume.

Pure Substances

  • Element: Contains only one type of atom.
  • Compound: Two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportion.

Mixtures

  • Homogeneous mixture: Uniform composition.
  • Heterogeneous mixture: Non-uniform composition.

3. Laws of Chemical Combination

Law of Conservation of Mass

Mass can neither be created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction.

Law of Definite Proportions

A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements in the same fixed proportion by mass.

Law of Multiple Proportions

When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole-number ratios.

4. Atomic Mass and Molecular Mass

Atomic mass is expressed relative to 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.

1 atomic mass unit (u) = 1/12 × mass of one carbon-12 atom

Molecular Mass

Molecular mass is the sum of atomic masses of all atoms present in one molecule.

Example:

For H₂O:

Molecular mass = 2(1) + 16 = 18 u

5. Mole Concept ⭐

A mole is the amount of substance containing the same number of entities as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.

1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles

The number 6.022 × 10²³ is called the Avogadro constant.

Important Formulae

Number of particles = Number of moles × NA
Number of moles = Number of particles / NA
Number of moles = Given mass / Molar mass
Given mass = Number of moles × Molar mass
Example:

How many molecules are present in 2 mol of H₂O?

Number of molecules = 2 × 6.022 × 10²³

= 1.2044 × 10²⁴ molecules

6. Molar Mass

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance.

Molar mass = Mass of substance / Number of moles

The common unit is g mol⁻¹.

Example:

Molar mass of CO₂:

= 12 + 2(16)

= 44 g mol⁻¹

7. Percentage Composition

Percentage composition tells us the percentage by mass of each element present in a compound.

% of element = (Mass of element in one mole of compound / Molar mass of compound) × 100
Example: Percentage of oxygen in H₂O

Molar mass of H₂O = 18 g mol⁻¹

Mass of oxygen = 16 g

% O = (16 / 18) × 100

≈ 88.89%

8. Empirical Formula

The empirical formula represents the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of different elements in a compound.

Basic Method

  1. Convert percentage into mass.
  2. Divide each mass by its atomic mass.
  3. Divide all values by the smallest value.
  4. Convert into the simplest whole-number ratio.
  5. Write the empirical formula.

9. Molecular Formula

Molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms of each element present in one molecule.

Molecular formula = (Empirical formula)n
n = Molecular mass / Empirical formula mass
Example:

If empirical formula = CH₂O and molecular mass = 180,

Empirical formula mass = 30

n = 180 / 30 = 6

Molecular formula = C₆H₁₂O₆

10. Stoichiometry ⭐

Stoichiometry deals with quantitative relationships between reactants and products in a chemical reaction.

Steps for Numerical Problems

  1. Write the balanced chemical equation.
  2. Convert given quantities into moles.
  3. Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation.
  4. Convert the answer into the required unit.
JEE Tip: Always balance the chemical equation before doing stoichiometric calculations.

11. Limiting Reagent

The reactant that gets completely consumed first and limits the amount of product formed is called the limiting reagent.

Remember: The limiting reagent determines the maximum amount of product that can form.

Basic Approach

  1. Calculate moles of each reactant.
  2. Compare them with their stoichiometric coefficients.
  3. Identify the reactant that produces the least amount of product.
  4. That reactant is the limiting reagent.

12. Concentration of Solutions

Molarity

Molarity (M) = Moles of solute / Volume of solution in litres

Molality

Molality (m) = Moles of solute / Mass of solvent in kg

Mass Percentage

Mass % = (Mass of solute / Mass of solution) × 100

Volume Percentage

Volume % = (Volume of solute / Volume of solution) × 100

Parts Per Million

ppm = (Number of parts of component / Total number of parts) × 10⁶
